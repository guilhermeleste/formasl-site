The question left by the problem of fragmentation can be stated simply: if we need to divide a reality in order to make it computable, how do we decide what can be left aside and what must survive the division? It is tempting to answer by starting with the model, as though the first task were to design a sufficiently general representation of the company and then fit customers, contracts, people, payments, and decisions into it. But the order was different. The company did not have to be imagined as a limiting case for testing how far a language or an architecture could go. It was already there. People work, customers buy, suppliers deliver, contracts bind parties, payments fulfill or fail to fulfill obligations, and decisions change what can happen next. It is over this already existing object that software tries to operate.

This point forces us to be precise about what we mean by the concrete. It is not merely what is material or physically observable. A piece of equipment is part of a company, but so is a contractual obligation; a building occupies space in an obvious way, while an institutional role exists differently and can still determine who may commit the organization in a transaction worth millions. A bank transfer can be observed as a financial movement, but what it means depends on the relation in which it occurred. When we ask what a company is, therefore, we do not find a collection of independent objects. We find people related to organizations, organizations related to one another, contracts producing commitments, decisions modifying possibilities, payments responding to obligations, and events acquiring meaning from the circumstances in which they occurred. This fabric exists before we choose how to represent it.

In the previous article we saw the result of a long specialization: different tools made aspects of the company computable, but distributed their representation across systems that later have to be reconnected. To understand what happened in that process, we need to retrace the path from the object those systems cut into pieces. Before the “CRM customer” there is a person or organization participating in a commercial relation; before the “authorized user” there is a person occupying a particular position under certain conditions; before the financial entry there is some event that explains why money should or should not change hands. Software categories appear when we select certain characteristics of these situations so that we can operate on them.

There is no way to avoid this movement. Every computation has to determine what it operates on and, in doing so, leaves something out. To calculate payroll we do not need to represent everything we know about each employee; to authenticate a person we do not need to reconstruct all of their commercial relations; to recognize revenue, accounting selects properties different from those that matter to the sales team. Abstraction makes it possible to reduce the richness of the object to a form over which some operation becomes possible. The difficulty is not reduction itself, but knowing which differences may disappear without compromising what we will need to distinguish later.

Suppose an application represents under a single state, “approved,” three situations that often travel together: a person had authority to authorize a particular operation, a decision was actually made, and the operation subsequently occurred. As long as everything works as expected, condensing them may seem sufficient. The difference appears when something leaves the normal path. The person had authority but never approved the operation; the decision was made, but execution failed; or the operation occurred without the required authorization. Three situations the model treated as equivalent begin to produce different questions and consequences. The simplification became inadequate not because simplifying is wrong, but because it eliminated a difference the world itself remained capable of presenting.

The same happens when what someone declares is confused with what occurred, or when a forecast and a later event are treated as versions of the same state. A customer claims to have made a payment that the responsible institution has not yet observed; two departments maintain incompatible information about an obligation; one person believes they are authorized to act and another disputes the source of that authorization. As long as all versions agree, representing their differences can look excessive. Error, conflict, delay, incomplete information, or a change of context reveals why they were not the same thing. The world does not commit itself to remaining inside the simplifications used to represent it.

This gives us a stricter criterion for evaluating an abstraction. It is not enough to ask whether it is simple, elegant, or convenient for an implementation; we also have to ask what we can still distinguish when we return from the model to the situations that gave rise to it. If two cases that should admit different decisions become indistinguishable, some relevant information has been lost. If a transformation preserves a calculated result but eliminates the possibility of knowing where it came from, it may be perfectly adequate for one purpose and insufficient for another. An abstraction must also be judged by what it allows us to reconstruct.

This perspective changes how we begin a system. A technology naturally suggests certain forms of representation: relational databases invite us to think in tables, graphs in nodes and relations, event-oriented architectures in events and messages. None of this makes those tools unsuitable; each may be the correct realization for a problem. The inversion occurs when we let the categories of the chosen mechanism decide in advance which domain differences deserve to exist. If a relation is necessary to distinguish two materially different situations, its presence in the model should not depend on whether it fits elegantly into the selected technology. First we need to discover what has to be preserved; the choice of how to realize it comes later.

Nor do we discover all these requirements at once. A first model may represent people and organizations and appear sufficient until two people formally connected to the same organization produce different behavior because they occupy different roles. We add roles. Then we notice that the same role produces different effects under different contracts or policies, and we begin representing those conditions. Later a temporal question appears: it is not enough to know what role someone holds today; we need to know which role they held when a particular decision was made. The representation becomes richer because new concrete cases show that differences previously ignored need to become computable.

This enrichment can continue without there ever being a point at which the model finally becomes confused with reality itself. A later representation may preserve more context than an earlier one, distinguish identities, relations, and events more effectively, and permit questions that were previously impossible; it remains a representation built for particular purposes. This distance is indispensable. A model can represent with great precision a false declaration, a hypothesis that will never be realized, or two incompatible versions of the same event. Its quality may lie precisely in keeping those positions distinct, not in silently deciding which one corresponds to the world. Increasing representational capacity is not the same as acquiring privileged access to truth.

When we repeatedly return to the company to test these abstractions, its own relations begin to enlarge the object. The organization buys from suppliers, sells to customers, employs people, takes credit, keeps accounts at banks, uses payment systems, pays taxes, operates under particular norms, and depends on infrastructure maintained by other institutions. A contract may involve parties in different jurisdictions; a financial decision depends on currency and banking institutions; a supplier, seen from another point of view, is another company with its own people, contracts, decisions, and obligations. What first appeared external begins to enter the representation because it participates in the conditions under which internal relations make sense.

A change of scale also changes the role of what is represented. A bank may initially appear only as infrastructure through which a company makes payments; when the object becomes the financial system, the bank becomes a participant, makes decisions, undertakes commitments, maintains relations with other institutions, and is subject to rules and authorities. A company that seemed to contain the entire world relevant to an application becomes only one participant when we look at an industry; the industry itself comes to occupy a similar position when we enlarge the scale again. The path did not begin by imagining an economy and trying to reduce it until reaching the company. It began with the concrete company and encountered economy, institutions, and social relations by following links that were already present within it.

It is from this movement that the original horizon of thinking about an economy—or, at its most ambitious limit, a society—emerges. This is a horizon of conception, not a claim that a complete computational model of these objects exists today, nor that any model could exhaust them. The methodological consequence is more restrained: if we begin from the concrete and follow the relations required to reconstruct it, there is no conceptual reason to decree in advance that those relations must stop at the administrative boundary of a company. The scale expands for as long as the object itself requires us to cross that boundary.

It was along this path that Promise Theory and Mark Burgess's work on semantic spacetime became especially useful. Promise Theory offered instruments for thinking about systems composed of autonomous participants whose relations need not be described from the point of view of a central controller that perfectly knows and determines the state of every part. Work on semantic spacetime developed questions of relation, position, context, and perspective in spaces composed of agents. The interest was not in importing from these theories a ready-made ontology for companies or societies, but in finding more precise ways to think about problems that had already appeared in the return to the concrete: different participants can know different things, occupy different positions, and relate to one another without an omniscient observer capable of replacing all those perspectives with a single privileged description.

From that point on, certain differences stop looking like exceptions that we add to the model only when a rare case demands them. If people and organizations can occupy different positions, if a declaration can diverge from an event, if a decision is not the same as its execution, if relations change over time, and if the same entity can play different roles depending on scale or context, then we need a systematic way to preserve these separations. The problem is no longer simply to find a schema large enough to store what we know, but to determine what cannot be fused without materially altering the world we want to make computable.

We began with a company because it was already there. We decomposed that concrete object because operating computationally over it required abstraction. When we returned to the cases, we discovered that some differences could be discarded while others reappeared precisely when situations became more difficult. Following their relations enlarged the object and made it even clearer that a representation able to survive different perspectives, contexts, and scales would need to preserve certain distinctions by construction.

The next question, therefore, is no longer which tools we should use to represent the world, but what world we must be able to represent so that computation does not destroy precisely the differences that led us to model it in the first place.
