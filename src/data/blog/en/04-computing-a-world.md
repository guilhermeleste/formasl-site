Building a computational world capable of distinguishing agents, relations, claims, contexts, times, norms, decisions, and events solves only half of the problem. A representation may carefully preserve all these differences and still remain passive: it records who participated in a situation, what was claimed, which rules were in force, and how the present state came to exist, but it does not by itself answer the questions that motivated its construction. We want to query this world, analyze it, derive consequences, verify conditions, compare possibilities, and, in some cases, make decisions that ultimately produce effects outside the representation itself. The next step is to make the modeled world the object of computation.

This transition introduces a new risk. In the previous articles we worked not to confuse a claim with truth, a decision with its execution, a hypothesis with the reference world, or technical capability with authority. If every computational operation ends by producing just another “fact,” those differences will disappear precisely when we begin to use them. The result of a query, an inference, an assessment, and a decision can all be stored with equal ease, even though each arose from a different operation and has a different status. Computing a semantically structured world requires that this difference continue to exist after the computation ends.

The simplest operation is to ask something that the representation already contains. In the commercial case from the previous article, we may want to know who occupied a particular role when a sale occurred, what claims exist about an authorization, which policy was in force, or which contract bound the parties. The query traverses relations and selects what satisfies particular conditions. Finding in the model that a person occupied a certain role during a certain period does not mean that the person themselves claimed this, that an authority confirmed the information, or that the system acquired privileged access to reality. It means only that the relation was found under the conditions of the query.

Other operations produce results that were not explicitly recorded. We can combine information, calculate properties, or derive consequences from the premises available. If a policy establishes a certain limit, a person occupied the role to which that limit applies, and an operation has a certain value, a reasoning mechanism may reach a relevant conclusion. The result arose from a derivation. If we preserve only the conclusion and erase the premises and the way it was obtained, we retain useful information while losing part of the meaning needed to assess it.

This difference stops looking abstract when we place the model inside an institution in operation. Consider a bank. A customer requests a transfer to another account. To someone who sees only the final effect, the operation appears simple: the amount was on one side and then appeared on the other. For the bank, however, the request opens a sequence of different questions. It is necessary to verify whether the received message has the expected structure, whether the accounts and contracts admit that type of operation, which limits or restrictions apply, whether the person making the request occupies the required position, and whether any rule requires additional approval. Depending on the case, it will also be necessary to determine who has authority to grant it and, after those checks, decide whether the institution will proceed.

These are not several ways of asking the same question. A request may satisfy certain formal conditions and still not be admissible under a policy. It may be admissible and require authorization. Authorization may exist without the final decision to execute having been made. If all of this is condensed into a single state called “approved,” the operation may continue to work in ordinary cases, but we lose the ability to reconstruct where it should have stopped, which condition was satisfied, who held authority, and who made the decision.

This is why, in an institutional world, computing does not mean merely querying or calculating. Some operations examine what is represented; others produce analyses or inferences; others verify normative conditions, admissibility, or authority; others record decisions, explore hypothetical situations, or request that some action be executed. These differences do not exist to multiply terminology. They exist because the represented world contains distinctions that would disappear if every transformation had the same semantics.

The difference becomes even clearer after a decision is made. Deciding that a transfer should occur does not move money. The decision may produce a request directed to some mechanism capable of executing it: a payment system, a ledger, or another specialized substrate. That mechanism receives what it needs to perform its function and attempts to produce the requested effect. It does not thereby need to become the authority that determined whether the operation should occur. It may be excellent at executing and recording movements without reconstructing on its own the contracts, policies, delegations, and reasons that gave the transfer its institutional meaning.

Nor does execution necessarily end the story. An order may be accepted and fail later; a movement may be recorded in one component while still awaiting settlement in another; settlement may occur before the application responsible for the decision receives confirmation. Execution, the effect produced, and the observation of that effect may occupy different moments. When information returns to the represented world, it can be related to the request, the conditions examined, the decision, and the mechanism that tried to execute it. What returns remains an observation about what occurred, not a retrospective fusion of the whole chain into a single event.

The transfer therefore reveals a structure that the final result normally hides. There is a request; certain conditions are examined; there may be validation, admission, and authorization; some decision is made; that decision may produce an execution request; specialized mechanisms try to produce an effect; and later observations make it possible to incorporate what happened into the history of the world. Not every real operation has to pass rigidly through this sequence. What matters is that, when these differences exist, computation should not eliminate them merely because their results pass through the same system.

This way of organizing the problem also changes the relation with the specialized tools that appeared in the first article. A ledger may remain focused on entries and balances; a payment system, on transferring value; a risk model, on producing assessments. None needs to be redesigned to contain the entire company on its own. Specialization remains advantageous. The question becomes whether what each mechanism produces can remain related to the broader world in which it acquired meaning.

An artificial-intelligence system is a good test of this separation. A model may analyze documents, estimate risk, recommend a response, or suggest an action. An agent may possess the technical capability to initiate a request. None of this grants it institutional authority by itself. A recommendation remains a recommendation unless some rule or decision assigns it another role. The more powerful a computational capability becomes, the more important it is to know what function it actually occupies in the chain, because the capability to produce a result and authority to decide are different properties.

The same principle appears in normative contexts. A legal text does not become a computational decision directly merely because it has been digitized or formalized. Between a normative act that exists in the world and a concrete action there may be interpretation, formalization, rules applicable to particular circumstances, validation or authorization processes, decisions, and executions. A formalization does not become the law itself, just as a formalized rule does not automatically become a decision. The example matters not because all legal activity can be mechanized, but because it shows with particular clarity what is lost when semantically different stages are treated as a single operation.

So far, however, we have operated on what we take to be the current world. Institutions also need to reason about what has not yet happened. A new policy may be considered before it enters into force; economic conditions may change; alternatives must be compared before one is chosen. The distinction between reference world and hypothesis, preserved in the model, now takes on a computational function.

Suppose the bank wants to study a policy under which transfers above a certain value would require an additional form of confirmation. We do not need to modify the policy currently in force in order to examine some consequences of the proposal. We can build a hypothetical context in which the new condition holds and, within it, ask which operations would be affected, analyze patterns, derive consequences, or verify how other rules would interact with the change. The results belong to that scenario. Exploring a possibility does not make it happen, and a consequence derived under a hypothesis should not silently appear as a condition of the current world.

The ability to operate over scenarios also lets us return to the change of scale introduced earlier. So far the bank has functioned as the principal world: within it there are customers, accounts, contracts, policies, decisions, transfers, and observations. Seen from outside, the bank itself participates in relations with other financial institutions, payment systems, regulators, markets, and monetary authorities. What once contained almost the entire relevant world becomes, at a larger scale, only one of its agents.

We can imagine a systemic representation in which multiple banks and other institutions participate in the same financial world. An institution such as a central bank could operate a much broader view of this representation than any individual bank. It would still be a view of the available model, not of the economy itself. More information and greater scope do not produce omniscience: missing data, partial observations, imperfect models, and events the system does not yet know about continue to exist.

At this level, scenarios take on another dimension. If the monetary-policy rate in the reference world is 10%, we can construct hypothetical contexts in which it appears at 8%, 10%, or 12% and examine what the available models produce under each set of conditions. We can query relations, perform analyses, derive consequences, verify constraints, and compare results without allowing any of these hypothetical worlds to replace the current state automatically.

Nothing requires all these calculations to be performed by a single mechanism. An econometric model may answer some questions; an agent-based simulator, others; an optimizer or a machine-learning model may participate in specific parts of the computation. Computational capability may vary according to the problem. What should not vary silently is the meaning of the conditions under which the result was produced: which scenario it belongs to, which premises it used, which mechanism participated, and what kind of result was obtained.

This also marks the limit of the example. A representation that includes more banks, companies, credit, contracts, sectors, regions, policies, and infrastructure may allow more detailed questions. It does not guarantee true predictions. A scenario may be perfectly coherent with inadequate premises; a sophisticated model may calculate with precision relations that represent the economy poorly; essential information may be absent. Increasing the resolution of the representation expands what we can compute; it does not eliminate the difference between a computational result and reality.

The same discipline applies to decisions. Analyses may change our assessment of the alternatives; inferences may reveal consequences; checks may show that certain conditions have been satisfied; scenarios may allow comparisons that were previously impossible. Even so, when an institution finally decides something, it still matters who made the decision, under what authority, in what context, and on the basis of which elements. If an execution request then arises, we cross another boundary. The chain can continue to advance without each stage retroactively acquiring the status of the next.

The computational world then acquires a dynamics that did not exist while we were merely describing it. New information enters; queries recover relations; analyses transform data; inferences establish consequences; normative operations verify conditions and authorities; scenarios open hypothetical worlds; decisions guide actions; execution requests reach specialized mechanisms; observations return and change what can be known afterward. Each operation receives something, produces something, and leaves some relation between what it received and what it produced.

It is precisely this relation that begins to become problematic.

An analysis may retain its final number and lose the data or conditions that produced it. An inference may preserve the conclusion and lose the premises. A risk recommendation may pass through different services and reappear as though it were a decision. A result calculated in a hypothetical scenario may be copied into the current world without carrying the hypothesis on which it depended. An execution may return only “success,” making it difficult to reconstruct which decision motivated it, who held authority, or what effect was later observed. Each component may have perfectly satisfied its technical contract and yet the meaning of the chain may deteriorate.

This is a different problem from the fragmentation with which we began the series. There, the world was decomposed among specialized systems and we had to recover the relations among their cuts. Here, the relations already exist in the model, but they can disappear during computation itself. We carefully preserve claim and truth, authorization and decision, decision and execution, hypothesis and current world; then a sequence of transformations can once again reduce everything to results without genealogy.

It is therefore not enough for the distinctions to be present at the input. They must remain recoverable after the world has been queried, transformed, analyzed, inferred over, compared, decided upon, and executed. Otherwise, the more we compute over a rich representation, the greater the distance may become between the result we receive and the structure that made it possible to understand what that result meant.

Computing a world therefore requires an additional property. Meaning cannot exist only before computation or depend on artisanal reconstruction after it.

Meaning must survive computation.
