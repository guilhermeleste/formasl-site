In the previous article we arrived at a question that can no longer be answered merely by choosing a technology or designing a data schema: what kind of world must we be able to represent so that computation does not erase precisely the differences we found in the concrete? The word “world” may sound expansive while we are still speaking about companies, contracts, payments, and decisions, but it denotes something quite precise. If we want to reconstruct a situation computationally, we need to represent not only the things involved, but also the relations among them, the participants who act, the positions they occupy, what they know or claim, the conditions under which certain relations hold, the moments when they change, what is permitted or required, and the history that explains how the present situation came to exist. The object stops looking like a collection of records and begins to require a structure of its own.

Consider a situation similar to those in the previous articles. An employee negotiates a nonstandard commercial condition and claims to have received authorization to grant it. The sale proceeds. Some time later, another person disputes that the authorization was ever given. There is an approval record in a system, an exchange of messages among those involved, a policy defining commercial limits, and, later, a payment made by the customer. As long as no one questions the process, it may be enough to say that the operation was approved and completed. Once a dispute arises, however, that description begins to break apart into questions: who claimed that authorization existed? Who was able to grant it? Was the person who recorded the approval also the person who had authority to decide? Was the policy in force at that time? Does the payment demonstrate that money moved, or does it also prove that what preceded it was valid? Was what we know now already known by the same people when the decision was made?

The first difficulty is not to confuse what the system contains with reality itself. A person may claim something true or false; a record may be correct or incorrect; an observation may be partial; an interpretation may change after new information appears. Reality does not begin obeying the database because some information was written into it. If everything that enters the system automatically acquires the status of true fact, the possibility of representing error, fraud, divergence, uncertainty, lack of knowledge, or revision disappears. What exists, what someone claims exists, what was observed, and what is concluded from those observations may coincide, but they do not have to.

This prevents the world from being built implicitly from the point of view of an observer who knows everything. In the commercial operation, the customer knows that a payment was initiated; the bank knows that it received a particular instruction; finance may know only that the amount has not yet been reconciled; the salesperson knows the negotiation; the compliance function knows a policy that the others may never have consulted. Each participant occupies a different position and has access to a different part of the situation. None of them has to be wrong for their descriptions to differ, and none becomes omniscient merely because its view is broader than the others'.

It is in this sense that agency becomes part of the model. An agent does not need to be an artificial intelligence. It may be a person, an organization, an institution, or another participant whose identity and position are relevant to the domain. Agents may undertake commitments, make claims, observe events, possess capabilities, make decisions, and participate in relations without thereby becoming universal authorities on what is true. Even an organization able to assemble a global view of the computational world it manages still sees a representation constructed from the information available. Having access to the entire model is not equivalent to having unrestricted access to the reality the model seeks to represent.

When one of these participants claims something, the identity of the speaker starts to matter. Suppose the salesperson says that authorization was received while their superior claims never to have granted it. If the system retains only an `authorized = true` or `authorized = false` field, it will have to choose one version or replace one with the other. The concrete situation, however, contains something neither value preserves on its own: two claims attributable to different participants. The disagreement may be exactly the information we need to preserve.

This need gives rise to a category such as Claim: an identifiable, situated assertion attributable to someone. A Claim may state that an authorization was granted, that a particular contract was in force, or that a payment occurred. Its presence in the model records that this content was asserted under certain conditions; it does not turn the content into truth. Two incompatible claims can therefore coexist without the system having to eliminate one immediately in order to recover an appearance of consistency. The conflict remains represented as conflict.

The same care applies to observations and assessments. An institution may observe a payment; an employee may report what they believe happened; a later audit may examine the available information and reach another conclusion. These elements may support one another or conflict, but they perform different functions. When something goes wrong, we often need to reconstruct who knew what, who claimed what, on the basis of which information, and how a particular conclusion was reached. Merging all these positions into a single “true state” simplifies precisely the cases in which the distinction matters most.

The relations in which these participants act do not exist without context either. Saying that someone “may approve discounts” seems clear enough until we ask: for which company, in which role, under which policy, up to what amount, or in which jurisdiction? A person may exercise authority in one subsidiary and none in another; may hold a particular role only within one project; may have different limits depending on the operation; may be subject to different rules when the region or institutional setting changes. A relation does not first receive a complete meaning and then acquire context as metadata. In many cases, the conditions under which it holds are part of the very meaning we are trying to represent.

The same applies to the idea of position. Some applications genuinely need coordinates, distances, maps, and spatial reference systems. Others need to know whether someone is within a jurisdiction, an organizational unit, an institutional domain, or an operational region. There is no reason to turn latitude, longitude, kilometers, or any particular geometry into universal properties of everything that exists merely because certain problems depend on them. The model needs to preserve what is semantically relevant about position and scope; when physical geometry is necessary, specialized mechanisms can realize it without redefining the meaning of the represented relations.

Time demands the same discipline. An operation may occur on Monday, be observed on Tuesday, become known to a person on Wednesday, and be officially recorded on Thursday. A policy may be in force when the event occurs and already have been replaced when the case is analyzed. A decision may precede its execution; the effect produced by the execution may arise later; the observation of that effect may arrive later still. In trivial situations, all these moments may coincide, making a single timestamp seem sufficient. Once they cease to coincide, we discover that “when?” was not a single question.

Imagine that the employee involved in the negotiation had authority to approve a certain discount until the end of June and lost that authority in July. Querying only the current state would answer that the employee does not have authority; it would not answer whether they had it when the sale occurred. Likewise, information discovered in an audit in August cannot be treated retroactively as something the participants knew in June. The time of occurrence, the validity period of a rule, the moment of decision, the moment of observation, and the moment when someone came to know certain information must be able to diverge without the present silently rewriting the conditions under which the past took place.

Up to this point we might imagine that we are merely enriching a description. Companies and institutions, however, are not made only of what happens. They also contain capabilities, permissions, obligations, and authorities. A person may be technically capable of making a transfer because they possess a valid credential and still lack permission to make it. They may have permission and no obligation. An obligation may exist and the action may never occur. A prohibited operation may happen while remaining prohibited. The institutional world contains normative relations that condition action without mechanically determining what will occur.

This is why executing something does not prove that there was authority to execute it. A software component capable of triggering an operation does not become the authority that decides whether the operation should occur. An employee may have technical access to a resource without having institutional power to use it in that case. A competent person may make a valid decision and the execution may still fail. The execution may produce a particular effect, and only later may some participant observe that the effect occurred. Decision, execution, effect, and observation belong to the same history, but they are not interchangeable.

The initial negotiation can now be reconstructed with greater precision. The salesperson may have been technically able to record the discount while being authorized only to request an exception. Another person may have held authority to decide. That decision may actually have been made or merely claimed to have been made; it may have been made and never executed; the operation may have occurred without a valid decision; the later payment demonstrates that a certain economic effect occurred, but it does not automatically legitimize all the preceding steps. A sequence that in a simple application might appear as `pending → approved → completed` unfolds because the organization itself distinguishes capability, permission, authority, decision, execution, and occurrence.

Nor does everything an organization needs to represent belong to the world it takes as current. Companies continuously work with possibilities: budgets, forecasts, assumptions, alternative courses of action, policies still under proposal. We may want to ask what would happen if a certain condition changed without turning that condition into reality merely by introducing it into an analysis. A hypothesis about rising defaults should not silently modify the current situation of customers; a new policy tested in a scenario should not begin governing real operations because it was included in a simulation. The hypothetical must be able to exist as hypothetical.

This separation is also part of history. A policy may be proposed, tested, approved, enter into force, and later be revised. The current text does not turn the previous one into something that never existed. A person may withdraw a claim without erasing the fact that they made it. A new observation may alter an earlier assessment without making it impossible to reconstruct why the earlier assessment seemed reasonable given the information available at the time. Revision changes the situation; it need not erase the genealogy that produced it.

A computational world capable of answering historical questions therefore cannot be merely a snapshot continuously overwritten by the latest known state. Many questions depend precisely on the path: which rule was in force, who possessed certain information, which decision preceded a particular execution, what effect was produced, who observed it, and what new evidence motivated a revision. The present state remains important, but it ceases to be sufficient when understanding the present requires knowing how it came to exist.

What emerges from these requirements is very different from a collection of entities accompanied by attributes. We have situated participants with partial access to the world; they make attributable claims; they observe and assess events; they may disagree without the conflict having to be erased; they participate in relations whose meaning depends on context, scope, and different temporal roles; they exercise capabilities and authorities that are not the same as what actually occurs; they consider possibilities without modifying the reference world; and they leave behind a history of decisions, effects, observations, and revisions. Some of these structures, in turn, become objects of other relations: one claim may refer to another, an observation may support an assessment, a rule may govern a particular relation, and a decision may depend on prior information or decisions.

The need for a representation capable of accommodating all of this did not arise from a preference for a particular kind of database. It arose from the object itself. Many of the relations encountered have more than two participants, and the roles played by each participant are part of the meaning. In an authorization, for example, it matters to distinguish who grants it, who receives it, which capability is being authorized, and under what conditions. In a decision, it may matter who decides, what is being decided, on the basis of which elements, and under what authority. We can decompose these structures into smaller relations, but if we do, some mechanism will have to preserve that they belong to the same event or relation and that their participants do not occupy interchangeable positions. The relation itself therefore needs enough identity for us to qualify it, make claims about it, or make it participate in larger structures.

It is only after encountering these requirements that the current logical representation chosen for FormaSL appears: a typed role-incidence metagraph. The name is more difficult than the idea. Instead of requiring every relation to be reduced to a binary edge with no identity of its own, a relation can be treated as an identifiable element in which different participants are incident through semantically distinct roles. That relation can in turn participate in other structures. Claims, observations, decisions, and other elements remain distinguishable and relatable without each new layer forcing the identity of the previous one to dissolve.

The metagraph does not determine what things the world must contain. The order was precisely the reverse. Requirements such as identity, roles, relations with multiple participants, context, history, and the possibility of establishing relations over relations came first; only then did it become necessary to choose a logical representation capable of preserving them. The metagraph is the form currently selected to play that role.

Nor does this choice require physical storage to be a graph database. The metagraph belongs to the logical representation, while physical realization may use relational structures, key/value structures, graph-oriented storage, or other mechanisms, provided that semantically relevant distinctions remain preservable and reconstructible. Changing the substrate should not change the meaning of who authorized something, what role someone held, which Claim was issued, or which relation was in force. The mechanism serves the model; it does not retrospectively redefine what the model means.

Seen this way, the choice of metagraph is the consequence of a path that began long before any decision about graphs. We began with a concrete company and discovered that different participants do not necessarily share the same view; that an assertion does not become true merely by existing; that relations depend on context, position, and time; that norms are not occurrences; that decisions, executions, effects, and observations must remain separable; that hypotheses can exist without modifying the reference world; and that history should not disappear whenever the present changes. Making all of this computable requires a representation in which identities, roles, and complex relations do not have to be reinvented only when a difficult question reveals that they were lost.

The result is still only a model. It does not observe reality from an absolute position, resolve disagreements by magic, convert assertions into truths, or grant any agent a privileged perspective over everything that exists. Its ambition is more precise: to keep available to computation differences that the world remains capable of presenting, even when a simpler abstraction would prefer to treat them as the same thing.

So far, however, we have dealt mainly with what this world needs to be able to represent. A model this structured would be little more than a sophisticated description if it remained passive. The next question is what happens when the model itself becomes a direct object of computation: when we want to query it, analyze it, infer consequences, verify conditions, compare possibilities, make decisions, and relate those decisions to the effects they produce.
