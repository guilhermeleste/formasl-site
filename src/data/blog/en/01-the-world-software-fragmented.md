A company does not begin in software. Before any record exists, people are already working, customers are buying, suppliers are delivering, contracts are creating obligations, money is moving in and out, decisions are being made, and events are leaving consequences. Some people may undertake commitments on behalf of the organization; others may not. Some payments are due, others are disputed. There is information known by some and unknown by others, deadlines still in the future, events already concluded, and a history that constrains what can happen next. None of these things comes into existence when a system is installed. A CRM does not create the customer it records, just as a finance system does not create the debt it tracks. The tools come later, because some part of this reality has to be represented with enough precision for us to record, calculate, query, verify, coordinate, or automate something about it.

Once we begin observing the company through these tools, however, that order almost disappears. What we see are customers in the CRM, employees in the human-resources system, identities in access control, contracts in a repository, assets and orders in the ERP, payments in finance, entries in accounting, processes in workflow tools, and indicators on an analytics platform. Each system has its own objects, states, identifiers, rules, and ways of organizing what it knows. After a while, these divisions begin to look as though they belonged to the company itself. We speak of the system user, the CRM customer, the ERP supplier, or the account holder as though these categories arrived ready-made from the world and software merely recorded them. In fact, they result from choices made to enable particular operations. A customer is not a CRM entity by nature; the customer appears that way because, for a particular purpose, we select some of its properties and relations and temporarily leave others aside.

Useful business computing would not exist without this kind of reduction. An accountant cannot produce a balance sheet by treating everything that happens in an organization as indistinguishable; revenue, expenses, assets, liabilities, periods, and accounting criteria have to be separated. An authentication mechanism selects identities, credentials, and resources. A sales team tracks customers, opportunities, and negotiations. Payroll represents certain properties of the same people who appear in the organizational chart and in the access-control system. Each activity requires a cut that can provisionally remove part of the complexity so that we can operate precisely on what matters. It would be of little use to require all these tools to carry, at all times, everything we know about the company. Specialization works precisely because it does not do that: it selects what it needs to distinguish, builds an appropriate representation, and makes certain operations manageable. In this sense, dividing the company into computational perspectives is not an accident we could simply avoid; it is one of the reasons we are able to compute over it.

The difficulty appears when we forget that what was separated for computational purposes remains related outside the systems. The person recorded by HR and the identity authenticated by access control are still the same person. The customer tracked by the CRM may also be the payer known to finance and the signatory of a contract stored elsewhere. Whether an employee may grant a particular discount can depend on their role, the commercial policy in force, the applicable contract, and the circumstances of that negotiation. Each piece of information may have good reasons to live in a different tool, but the relation among them does not disappear because of that. We gain precision by looking separately at each aspect of the company and, in exchange, take on the work of recovering their connections whenever a question crosses more than one of those perspectives.

An ordinary sale is enough to show what happens. A customer negotiates the purchase of a product or service and asks for a nonstandard condition. The employee responsible for the account may be able to submit the request but perhaps not approve it. To decide, it will be necessary to consider the amount involved, the commercial terms, the contract, and the role held at that moment by whoever participates in the approval. If the negotiation moves forward, an obligation will arise, then a charge, and at some point a payment; there may also be delivery, accounting recognition, and changes to commercial forecasts. For someone following the operation inside the company, there is an intelligible continuity among these events. The negotiation led to a special condition, the condition had to be approved, the sale produced an obligation, and that obligation ended in a payment. Across the software infrastructure, however, the same path runs through several places: the negotiation may be in the CRM, the employee's role in HR, their identity in the access system, the commercial policy in another service or document, the contract in another repository, the charge in finance, and the payment in banking infrastructure. Accounting will still produce its own representation of what happened.

This distribution causes little difficulty as long as we want to know only what each system was designed to answer. The problem appears when the question follows the real situation rather than the boundary of the application. Knowing whether a particular person could grant that discount requires more than locating an identifier or confirming that the person was authenticated: we need to know what role they held, what conditions were in force, which operation those conditions applied to, and when all of this occurred. Asking why a payment was made also goes beyond the financial entry, because the amount may be fulfilling an obligation that arose from a contract, a negotiation, and earlier decisions. The system that records the payment may correctly describe what happened without, by itself, possessing everything needed to explain why it happened that way. No error needs to have occurred. The applications merely answered the questions they were built to answer; it is the broader question that requires relations distributed among them to be recomposed.

Much of integration engineering exists for this reason. We share identifiers, create APIs, publish events, replicate data, establish contracts between services, build pipelines, consolidate information, and define which system should serve as the reference for particular records. These solutions are indispensable in an infrastructure composed of specialized tools. But part of what we do through them can be described more precisely as reconstruction. When we declare that the `customer_id` in one application corresponds to the account holder recorded in another, we computationally re-establish an identity that existed before either application. When we combine role, financial limit, and operation type to evaluate an approval, we recompose a relation that crosses different systems. When we correlate sale, invoice, payment, and accounting entry, we try to recognize that records produced by different tools at different times belong to the same chain of events. Integration transports data, but it must also recover the relations that allow those data to be interpreted together.

This difference matters because a technical connection, however necessary, does not automatically contain the meaning of what it connects. An API may correctly report that an operation was approved without saying where the approving person's authority came from, under what conditions it held, or until when it remained valid. An event may precisely record the completion of a payment while saying nothing about the obligation that payment fulfilled. Two systems may share the same person identifier and still represent very different roles held by that person. Placing these records side by side solves a problem of access; understanding the relation among them requires something more. As questions stop dealing only with isolated objects and begin to depend on context, sequence, responsibility, or links among events, this second kind of work becomes increasingly visible.

New layers help perform it. A data warehouse brings dispersed information together; an event architecture makes changes observable; an enterprise model reduces incompatibilities; a central service may concentrate rules that previously appeared in different applications. In many cases, these are precisely the right solutions. Even so, each new layer must decide which correspondences to establish, which information to preserve, and how to represent relations that originated under different models. Physically consolidating data does not mean that every connection needed to interpret them is now explicit. An organization may possess the records it needs and still depend on code, configurations, documents, policies, contracts, or accumulated human knowledge to reconstruct a situation. The information exists, but the relation among its parts may be distributed across many places—and sometimes remain only implicit.

As long as the question remains confined to a specialized function, this may not be a problem. Finance calculates balances without needing to know the career history of the person who approved an expense; the access system authenticates someone without reconstructing their commercial relation with customers; the CRM tracks opportunities without representing the company's accounting structure. This independence is useful. The difficulty grows when we need to know who could perform a particular action at that moment, which commitment produced an obligation, which decision led to a payment, or what changed between the situation in which a decision was made and the current situation. Questions like these traverse relations among people, contracts, events, rules, and different moments in time. The company does not reorganize those relations to match the architecture of its systems, so the answer often has to cross boundaries that were useful for building the software but do not belong to the object about which we want to reason.

Seen from this perspective, ERP, CRM, financial systems, access control, and analytics tools stop looking like pieces that, once added together, would form a complete digital company. Each can be understood as a computational perspective on a reality that precedes all of them. Accounting selects what it needs in order to perform its function; the CRM selects another part; the identity system, another. There is no reason to require any one of these representations to contain the entire company. A balance sheet does not need to carry employees' biographies, just as authentication does not need to reconstruct every commercial relation of the authenticated person. The difficulty begins when the independence of these representations is mistaken for independence of what they represent. Within the company, the same person can occupy different positions and be responsible for different actions; a contract can change what that person may do; a decision can trigger a payment; the same event can produce commercial, administrative, and accounting consequences. The tools separate perspectives that remain connected in the company.

Nor would we solve the issue simply by replacing many applications with one gigantic application. A central database would still have to decide how to represent a person, a contract, an authorization, a decision, or an event, and which relations among these things would remain explicit. Centralizing infrastructure changes where information is stored and where operations are executed; it does not eliminate the problem of choosing what to represent, nor does it guarantee that what was left out can be reconstructed later. The decisive question, therefore, is not how many databases exist or how many services take part in an operation. It lies in the passage between the company, which we encounter as a complex and interconnected reality, and the specialized abstractions we construct in order to work computationally over parts of it.

The better these specializations become, the more precisely we can operate on particular aspects of the organization. That gain is real and need not be abandoned. But as we want to ask questions and make decisions that cross those aspects, the importance of the relations that were divided among the representations also grows. The challenge is not to undo the decomposition, but to understand how to perform it without turning each perspective into an island whose connection to the others must be reinvented whenever the company once again requires a joint answer. Before asking how to integrate abstractions we have already built, we may need to step back to a more fundamental question: when we divide a reality to make it computable, what must we preserve so that we can still reconstruct it as the same reality?
