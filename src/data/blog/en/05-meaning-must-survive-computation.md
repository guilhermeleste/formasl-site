In the previous article we arrived at an uncomfortable consequence. We built a world capable of distinguishing claim from truth, hypothesis from current situation, authorization from decision, and decision from execution; then we began operating on it and discovered that computation itself can undo those differences. An inference retains its conclusion and loses its premises, an assessment passes through several services and reappears as a decision, a result produced inside a scenario loses the hypothesis on which it depended. No component needs to have failed technically. Even so, what reaches the end can no longer be interpreted with the same precision as what entered.

The most obvious response would be to carry all information through every stage, but that would make semantic preservation nearly incompatible with computation. Analyses aggregate, compilers transform representations, databases create indexes, distributed systems partition information, algorithms discard intermediate states. An operation does not need to reproduce everything it received in full in order to remain correct. What cannot disappear silently is what we will still depend on to understand the result, verify its use, or continue computing from it.

This is the requirement we call Semantic Continuity: information that is semantically relevant to the meaning or verification of a result must remain reconstructible across the transformations through which it passes. Reconstructible is the decisive word. The information may remain directly in the result, remain accessible by reference, or be recoverable from dependencies whose preservation is guaranteed. It is not necessary to copy everything everywhere; it is necessary that what still matters not become irrecoverable merely because the representation changed.

Consider a risk assessment produced during the analysis of a transfer. Its result might simply be a category such as high risk. Suppose, however, that this classification was calculated by a particular version of a model, from certain information, and within a scenario in which some hypothetical premises were active. We may copy high risk perfectly from one component to another and still lose meaning if, at the destination, it is no longer possible to reconstruct that it was a calculated result, under what conditions it was obtained, or from which materially relevant dependencies it arose.

The reverse is also true. A representation may change radically without its meaning changing. A large structure may be replaced by a reference that allows it to be recovered; an index may reorganize access; a compiler may produce an executable form different from the source. Semantic Continuity does not require an identity transformation or universal retention. It requires that changes in how something is represented not become, without explicit declaration, changes in what it means.

This changes the question asked at every computational boundary. We do not need to know whether absolutely all data survived, but which distinctions remain necessary after that operation. Some information may legitimately be discarded; other information may be transformed, restricted to a particular scope, or replaced by structures from which it can still be derived. Loss stops being an incidental implementation detail when it makes it impossible to recover a difference the contract still considers material.

Return to the bank transfer. After it is complete, it probably does not matter to preserve every temporary structure used by the components that participated in execution. It may matter, however, to be able to reconstruct who requested the operation, which decision led to execution, what authority supported that decision, which policy applied, which mechanism attempted to produce the effect, and which later observation supports what we know about the result. In another context, part of this information may be dispensable. The point is that this decision must follow from the semantic contract of the transformation, not from the accidental fact that an intermediate format has nowhere to carry it.

The defect appears when two situations that should remain different become indistinguishable. A recommendation produced by a model and an institutional decision may contain exactly the same text without being the same thing. A Claim and a proposition treated as true may have the same content and still occupy different semantic positions. “Unknown” and “false” may look equivalent to a poorly designed Boolean condition even though they describe radically different states. A hypothetical result may be structurally identical to another result belonging to the current world while still depending on the premises of the scenario in which it was produced.

When a transformation eliminates a difference of this kind without authorization, semantic erasure occurs. This is not simply the absence of desirable metadata. Two states that should remain distinguishable under the contract begin to produce the same observable representation for later computation. From that point on, subsequent operations may behave as though recommendation were decision, absence of knowledge were falsity, or hypothesis were occurrence. Semantic erasure is therefore a correctness defect.

The problem becomes more difficult when operations are composed. A first transformation may preserve perfectly the qualifications it received; a second may consume them correctly for its local purpose; a third may finally discard them even though the result of the chain still depends on them. Each component may look correct when examined separately while the composition remains semantically incorrect. Local correctness is not enough when the meaning required by the result crosses multiple boundaries.

For this reason, derived information remains derived when used by another operation. A result produced under a hypothesis remains dependent on that hypothesis for as long as that dependency remains material. An interrupted computation does not become complete because a later component managed to use what it produced. Composition does not silently promote semantic categories. If a qualification needs to cease to exist, the transformation responsible must make that loss an explicit part of its contract rather than allowing it to disappear by accident.

Preserving these distinctions still does not answer another inevitable question. Given a result or a decision, we may need to know not only what it means now, but how it came to exist. What information entered the process? Which observations or Claims were considered? What analysis was performed? Which inference produced a particular conclusion? Which rule participated in an authorization? Who decided? Which mechanism executed the action, and which later observation was incorporated into the world?

This is the question of traceability. It is related to Semantic Continuity, but it is not the same thing. Continuity preserves what we still need in order to interpret a result correctly; traceability preserves its materially relevant genealogy. We can have a result whose present meaning is clear and still be unable to reconstruct its path. We can also have millions of lines of operational records about an execution and, somewhere among them, have lost the difference between an assessment and a decision. One property does not replace the other.

Genealogy itself requires distinctions. Provenance answers where something came from and which sources or contributions it depends on. Derivation records how a conclusion was obtained from certain premises according to some reasoning process. An operational trace records events of execution: components called, messages transmitted, stages performed, failures, and returns. These structures may be connected, but they should not be merged. An operational record does not demonstrate a derivation; knowing the provenance of information does not establish a causal relation; a derivation does not necessarily tell us how the calculation was physically executed. Likewise, an explanation does not become a proof, nor does a detailed history automatically become a certificate.

Treating genealogy this way means making it an architectural obligation wherever it is materially necessary. It does not mean claiming that every possible transformation in the current system already has complete end-to-end traceability. The ambition of broad traceability belongs to the genesis of the project; the publishable claim today is more precise: the architecture makes preservation of relevant semantic genealogy an explicit requirement, whose concrete coverage must be demonstrated for every case in which implementation is claimed.

There is, however, a condition prior to both continuity and traceability. To say that a transformation preserved meaning, we need to know which meaning was supposed to be preserved. To recognize that some implementation changed a rule, we need to distinguish a change of realization from a semantic change. If what the system means is distributed implicitly across code, databases, configurations, instructions provided to models, models themselves, and operational conventions, that distinction begins to disappear.

Imagine a policy under which only a particular role may authorize operations above a certain value. The rule is documented, but one service contains an additional condition that permits an exception. Elsewhere, a database trigger imposes a policy that does not appear in the declared model. An instruction supplied to an agent grants it an additional institutional capability; a feature flag silently changes a material rule; a machine-learning classifier initially used as a recommendation gradually comes to determine decisions without that change being incorporated into the semantic contract.

None of these mechanisms is problematic by nature. Conditions in code, triggers, instructions to models, configurations, and models can all be perfectly legitimate parts of a realization. The problem begins when one of them acquires the power to modify meaning, admissibility, or authority without that modification being represented in the contract that is supposed to govern the system. A shadow semantics then appears: effective enough to determine behavior, but invisible in the place where we believed meaning was declared.

This kind of divergence often grows through reasonable local decisions. An exception solves an urgent case, an optimization adds a condition, an operational policy is implemented directly at the point where it will be executed. After many such changes, the question “what does this system mean?” can no longer be answered by examining an identifiable contract; it requires reconstructing the behavior of the entire infrastructure. At that point, the realization has stopped merely executing the semantics and has begun to produce semantics on its own.

Semantic Continuity therefore needs authority. There must be a source against which we can distinguish optimization from redefinition, explicit transformation from accidental alteration, and authorized loss from semantic erasure. In the current design of the project, this role belongs to the `.fsl` contract: it is the authoritative source of the meaning declared by the system.

The qualification is indispensable. Semantic authority does not mean authority over truth. A Claim declared in the contract does not become true merely by being there; a formalization of a norm does not become the norm itself or the legal interpretation from which it originated; an action described as authorized in the model does not, for that reason alone, acquire physical or institutional authority over the external world. The `.fsl` governs what the declared contract means to the system. It does not govern reality.

This separation also constrains the execution environment and storage. A database write does not by itself redefine the semantic source; an execution result does not retroactively alter the meaning of the language; a planner, compiler, or engine may change how an operation is realized without acquiring authority to change what it represents. A realization may optimize, specialize, and execute the contract. Its freedom ends where an undeclared semantic redefinition would begin.

We therefore have an architecture in which freedom of realization depends on semantic obligations. Representations may change; different mechanisms may be composed; some information may be discarded; other information may be transformed. The requirement is that distinctions that remain necessary stay reconstructible, that their materially relevant genealogies can be recovered, and that any effective change in meaning be governed by the authority that declares that meaning.

The problem that opened the series has, at this point, acquired a much more precise form. We began by asking why a company appears fragmented across specialized representations. We followed that question to a world capable of preserving relations that those representations normally separate, made that world the object of computation, and discovered that computation itself must answer for the continuity of the differences on which it operates. We now also know that this continuity cannot depend on implicit conventions scattered across the implementation: meaning must exist as an identifiable contract.

If that contract must declare a world, its distinctions, and the operations that can act on it, and if it must remain the semantic authority even when the realization changes, then what remains is to make that semantics expressible in a form of its own.

That is where a language becomes necessary.
