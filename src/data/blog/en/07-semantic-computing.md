The previous article ended at a boundary. FormaSL can declare a world, its relations, Claims, contexts, hypotheses, rules, and operations; it can distinguish query from inference, authorization from decision, and decision from execution. But declaring the meaning of a computation does not imply possessing the best mechanism for realizing it. An inference may require a specialized reasoning mechanism, an analysis may use statistical methods or machine learning, a query may depend on access structures suited to the volume of data, and an execution may cross external systems. The language needs to say what these operations mean without being required to implement internally every mechanism they may come to require.

This is the architecture that Forma calls Semantic Computing.

The expression does not mean merely computing over data to which we assign some meaning. In that loose sense, almost any software could be described as semantic. The definition here is narrower: Semantic Computing is an architecture in which declared meaning participates directly in computation, has a source of authority distinguishable from the mechanisms that realize it, and must preserve its materially relevant differences as it passes through transformations, reasoning, governance, decision, realization, and observation.

The decisive point is the direction of dependency. In conventional systems, much of an application's effective meaning may end up distributed among tables, data structures, services, conditions in code, configurations, operational rules, and documentation. None of these things is problematic by nature. The difficulty arises when we need to reconstruct retrospectively, from their combination, what the system actually considers an entity, an authorization, a decision, or an occurrence. Under that regime, changing an implementation may also change, without explicit intent, the meaning it helped produce.

Semantic Computing reverses that relation. The semantic contract is established as the authority over declared meaning, and the realization must satisfy it. If the contract distinguishes a Claim from a derived consequence, a physical representation cannot merge them merely because they have similar content. If a result depends on a hypothetical scenario, an analysis mechanism cannot erase that dependency because its internal format does not represent it. If authorization and decision occupy different jurisdictions, an operational flow is not permitted to turn them into a single condition called “approved” merely because doing so simplifies implementation.

The realization remains free to choose how to do its work. What it cannot choose on its own is a different meaning for the problem it received.

An application can, in this sense, be read as the combination of four elements: semantic world, semantic contract, semantic operations, and realization profile. The world contains what we chose to represent; the contract establishes the distinctions and obligations that govern that representation; the operations state what kind of computation will be performed over it. The realization profile answers a different question: through which concrete mechanisms will the necessary capabilities be satisfied?

In the current design, this separation also appears in the artifacts. The `.fsl` declares meaning and remains the authoritative source of the semantic contract. The `.fsp` belongs to the realization layer. It can select mechanisms, capabilities, substrates, and policies needed to make a particular application work. Resolving a realization means binding these choices to an already established contract; it does not mean rewriting the contract so that it fits what the infrastructure is capable of doing.

The distinction remains valid after we produce something executable. Compiling, planning, indexing, or materializing an application may generate artifacts very different from its source. Some will exist only during execution; others may be persisted or reused. None retroactively becomes the authority over the meaning of the `.fsl` merely because it is closer to the machine. The executable representation realizes the contract; it does not replace it.

Storage is a good test of this architecture. At the logical-semantic level, FormaSL adopts a typed role-incidence metagraph. This means that relations, identities, participations, and qualifications need to preserve certain semantic properties. It does not mean that the world must physically exist as a graph identical to the logical representation.

A realization may encode these structures in relational tables, key/value pairs, specialized indexes, native graph structures, or other representations. A system based on PostgreSQL and services is not excluded by definition; redb or RocksDB may be appropriate substrates in particular realizations; another application may justify graph-oriented storage. The architectural question is not which of these families visually resembles the metagraph, but whether the realization can preserve or reconstruct what the contract requires.

The same reasoning applies to other computational capabilities. An inference operation may be realized by a mechanism dedicated to the reasoning required in that domain; an analysis may use another mechanism; an operational sequence may be organized by a directed acyclic graph where appropriate. The architecture does not require a universal machine capable of absorbing all these functions. It requires each selected capability to remain bound to the operation it is realizing and to the semantic obligations of that operation.

Machine learning makes this separation especially clear. An analysis operation may be realized by a model whose architecture, parameters, infrastructure, and inference strategy change over time; those changes may affect quality, cost, and latency and may require new validation, but they do not receive authority to redefine the semantic status of the result. If the contract says that the output is an assessment, high statistical confidence does not turn it into an institutional decision or grant the model an authority that belongs to another participant in the domain.

The same principle applies to other specialized capabilities. An operational sequence may be coordinated by a directed acyclic graph where appropriate, and certain distributed problems may justify coordination or consensus properties offered by a blockchain. A database, an inference mechanism, a statistical model, and a coordination mechanism occupy realization positions when they satisfy capabilities of the contract; their presence or absence does not by itself redefine the declared world.

This freedom is useful precisely because different problems require different mechanisms. An application can combine efficient storage, reasoning, statistical analysis, and interaction with external systems without deciding which of these capabilities must become the foundation of the entire architecture. Each component can remain specialized. The challenge becomes preventing the boundaries among them from reconstructing the semantic fragmentation from which we began.

This is where Semantic Continuity reappears as a conformance criterion for realization. A realization may transform, index, compress, and specialize representations, but whatever remains material to interpreting, verifying, or computing a result must survive directly, through a stable reference, or through guaranteed reconstruction. If a choice eliminates a distinction the contract still requires, we do not merely have another possible implementation: for that contract, we have a non-conforming realization.

This is the limit of architectural freedom. Storage, inference mechanisms, analysis, planning, distribution, and internal representations may vary; variation ceases to be legitimate when it produces unauthorized semantic erasure. It is this normative relation—explicit authority over meaning accompanied by obligations on realizations—that makes Semantic Computing more specific than the mere existence of an abstract model with interchangeable implementations.

An interface may guarantee that two implementations expose the same functions and still allow one of them to interpret a relevant distinction differently. Two mechanisms may return the same serialized type and produce semantically distinct results. Two databases may store the same fields and treat identity or absence in different ways. The conformance that matters here does not end at the format of input and output; it reaches the semantically observable properties the contract requires to be preserved.

A practical consequence follows. We can replace physical mechanisms without necessarily redefining the application semantically. This does not mean that every substitution is automatic or that two mechanisms are operationally equivalent. It may be necessary to migrate data, recalculate indexes, validate accuracy, review costs, or demonstrate new performance properties. What the architecture allows is to separate these changes of realization from changes in meaning itself. When only the infrastructure changes, the contract provides the reference for knowing what must remain true about the application after migration.

The logical metagraph must also be understood within this freedom. It does not have to be the internal format used by every algorithm. An analysis may project part of the world into temporary tables; a model may use tensors; a search mechanism may build its own indexes; a planner may produce a different operational structure. These transformations do not contradict the logical model as long as whatever must return to or remain relatable to the semantic world preserves the corresponding obligations.

The architecture does not answer performance or cost questions by itself. The fact that semantics is organized as a metagraph does not determine how much a query will cost, which storage will be most efficient at a particular scale, or how much it costs to preserve genealogies and qualifications. Different realization profiles may produce different trade-offs among memory, latency, reconstructibility, and operational complexity, and these properties need to be measured. The architecture establishes what an optimization may not destroy; it does not determine by definition which optimization is best.

Nor do we need to turn the name Semantic Computing into a historical claim larger than what we have established. Forma uses the term to designate this decomposition: explicitly governed semantic world and contract, semantically distinct operations, Semantic Continuity, and realizations able to vary under defined obligations. Determining the historical relation of this combination to other architectures, languages, or traditions requires a separate comparative effort. The architecture can be defined and evaluated by what it claims without depending on a declaration that nothing similar has ever existed.

The result is more concrete than a choice of terminology. The `.fsl` remains the authority over declared meaning; the `.fsp` selects ways to satisfy capabilities; execution and storage remain different layers; the logical metagraph does not require physical graph storage; specialized mechanisms can realize different parts of the computation; and none receives semantic authority merely because it executes an important part of the system. Continuity among these parts ceases to be a desirable convention and becomes part of the conditions of correctness.

This makes it possible to recover specialization without necessarily repeating the fragmentation that opened our investigation. A database can remain specialized in storing; a reasoning mechanism, in deriving; a model, in analyzing; an executor, in producing actions. None needs to represent the whole world by itself, nor do we need to turn them into a single technology in order to preserve unity. Unity comes to reside in the common semantic contract and in the obligations governing the passage of information among different realizations.

It is in this sense that Semantic Computing completes the inversion that began when we refused to take tools as the starting point. First we looked for what needed to be represented; then we built a world capable of preserving its differences; we made that world computable; we required meaning to survive transformations; and finally we separated authority over that meaning from the specialized mechanisms that realize it. The architecture does not eliminate technical diversity. It makes that diversity subordinate to a semantically explicit contract.

So far, however, we have described this architecture in abstract terms. We know the role of the language, the contract, the operations, and realization profiles; we know why storage, reasoning, models, and execution should not compete for the same authority. What remains is to show how Forma itself materializes this decomposition into concrete components, which responsibilities belong to each, and which relations are optional or necessary.

It is this concrete architecture that closes the series.
